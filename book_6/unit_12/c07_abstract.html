Teach: Refining Rhetorical Structures

Teacher notes

1. Address the specific errors identified in T1, focusing on Fronted Syntactic Alterations for Rhetorical Persuasion.
2. Clarify the form: 'Fronted element + clause matrix' (e.g., 'Crucial though X was, Y') and 'What + clause + is/was + focused element' (e.g., 'What we need is X').
3. Explain the meaning: isolating key variables, structuring persuasive corporate critiques, and injecting variable textual cohesion during advanced argumentation loops.
4. Use the concept_check_questions to ensure understanding:
- "Are we shifting standard syntax layers specifically to emphasize a critical operational parameter? (Yes)"
- "Does this structure highlight a singular analytical nuance over standard declarative descriptions? (Yes)"
5. Provide clear examples, contrasting them with standard declarative sentences.
6. Guide students through the controlled practice activity, encouraging them to apply the corrected structures.

Student handout

Mastering Persuasive Emphasis

In professional settings, how we present information can be as important as what we present. Certain structures allow us to emphasize key details, isolate critical variables, and make our arguments more persuasive and nuanced.

Consider the structures below, focusing on how they shift emphasis:

* Cleft Sentences (What-Clefts): Used to highlight specific information. Example: 'What we need to achieve is a significant cost reduction.' (Emphasizes 'cost reduction').
* Fronting (Adverbials/Adjectives): Used to bring certain elements to the front of a sentence for emphasis or to create a more formal tone. Example: 'Crucial though the data analysis was, the interpretation phase required additional expertise.' (Emphasizes 'crucial' and sets up a contrast).

Now, rewrite the following sentences to achieve greater rhetorical impact using these advanced structures. Aim to highlight the bolded elements by placing them in a position of emphasis.

T2

Test 2: Defending Your Vendor Choice

Teacher notes

1. Explain that students will apply the refined structures to a practical scenario.
2. Remind them of the situational_focus: justifying a vendor choice under scrutiny by senior management.
3. Encourage the deliberate use of fronted and cleft structures to emphasize key points and address concerns.
4. Monitor for accurate and appropriate use of the target language, as well as the overall rhetorical effectiveness and professional tone.
5. Provide feedback on both grammatical accuracy and the strategic use of persuasive language.
6. Sample model answer:
"What we thoroughly assessed was the vendor's unparalleled track record in scalable cloud solutions, a critical factor for our future growth. Crucial though their initial cost estimate appeared, the long-term savings from operational efficiency and reduced downtime significantly outweigh this. What management must understand is that this decision was not simply about cost, but about securing a robust, future-proof infrastructure."

Student handout

Formulating a Strategic Defense

You are preparing a concise statement to senior management, defending your choice of a new logistics vendor. You've received some skeptical feedback, and you need to highlight the core reasons for your decision, acknowledge potential challenges, and reinforce your confidence.

Write a short persuasive paragraph (approx. 80-100 words) to present to management. Ensure you:
1. Clearly state the primary benefit of your chosen vendor.
2. Address one key concern or challenge related to the choice.
3. Use at least two examples of either a fronted element structure or a 'What-cleft' sentence to add emphasis and rhetorical power to your **justification**.

Think about how you can use these structures to isolate key variables and structure your argument effectively.
