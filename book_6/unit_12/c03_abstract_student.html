Spotting What Works Better

In your current or past work, have you ever noticed a way something could be done better or more efficiently? Think about any routine task or system.

Discuss with your partner:
1. What was the task or system you're thinking of?
2. What was the problem or bottleneck you observed?
3. Briefly, what was your idea for improvement?
4. Why do you think your idea would make things better?

How to Present Your Idea

Read the conversation between two colleagues, Maria (Team Lead) and David (Logistics Coordinator), discussing a proposal for a new inventory system.

Maria: Good morning, David. Thanks for coming in. I understand you have some thoughts on our current inventory process?

David: Good morning, Maria. Yes, I do. I've been observing our workflow in the warehouse, particularly how we manage stock intake and dispatch. I've identified what I believe is a significant bottleneck affecting our overall efficiency.

Maria: Go on. What have you noticed?

David: Well, our manual data entry system for incoming shipments often leads to delays. It’s a time-consuming step, and sometimes errors occur, which then ripple through the entire tracking system. This directly impacts our ability to optimize stock levels and fulfill orders promptly.

Maria: So, what’s your proposal?

David: I've put together a brief proposal for implementing a new barcode scanning system. This would streamline the intake process significantly. Scanners would immediately update the central database, reducing manual effort and minimizing errors.

Maria: That sounds interesting. What are the key benefits you foresee?

David: The immediate benefit would be increased efficiency and accuracy in inventory updates. Over time, we'd see a measurable improvement in workflow, leading to faster stock turnaround. Critically, I anticipate a positive ROI through reduced labor costs associated with manual entry and fewer discrepancies. It would also free up staff for more value-added tasks.

Maria: So, you're confident this would lead to substantial improvements?

David: Absolutely. I believe it's a crucial step towards modernizing our operations and ensuring we remain competitive. I've got some initial figures if you'd like to see them.

Maria: Let's schedule another meeting. Bring those figures.

Discussion:
1. What problem did David identify in the current inventory process?
2. What was his proposed solution?
3. How did David explain the benefits of his proposal?
4. What phrases did David use to sound professional and persuasive?

Getting Ready to Persuade

Now, think about your own workplace or a hypothetical one. You need to identify an area for improvement and prepare a proposal. Use the columns below to organize your ideas and choose useful phrases. Take notes – you don't need to write full sentences.

Your Turn to Propose!

Work in pairs. You will present your proposal to your partner.

Student A: The Proposer
You are presenting your idea for improving a work process. Your goal is to clearly explain the problem, propose your solution, and convince your 'manager' of the benefits, using some of the key terms and phrases you prepared.

Student B: The Manager
You are listening to your colleague's proposal. Ask questions to understand the problem and the solution better. Think about how the proposal could impact the team, budget, and overall operations. You don't have to agree immediately, but listen openly and critically.

After the first presentation, switch roles!

Reflecting on Your Proposals

Peer Reflection:
Discuss with your partner:
1. How clear and convincing was your partner's proposal?
2. What was the strongest point of their argument?
3. What questions did you still have about their idea?
4. What went well when you presented your own proposal? What would you do differently next time?

Class Feedback:
Listen as your teacher gives feedback on the language used during the presentations. Pay attention to common areas for improvement and effective phrases that stood out.
