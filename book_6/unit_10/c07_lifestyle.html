7. Do not provide answers or explicit teaching at this stage.

TCH

Teach: Clarifying Substitution and Ellipsis

Teacher notes

1. Review student answers from T1, highlighting common errors and successful attempts on the board.
2. Introduce the concept of substitution and ellipsis, explaining their purpose: avoiding repetition for conciseness and flow.
3. Present the target forms:
- Subject + auxiliary verb + do so/not/too
- Subject + omitted clause (e.g., I hope not / If not)
4. Use the provided concept check questions (CCQs) to ensure understanding:
- 'Are we removing or substituting words to prevent unnecessary structural repetition?' (Yes)
- 'Does the phrase 'do so' replace a complete active verb block from the previous statement?' (Yes)
5. Address specific errors from T1. For example, 'did not embraced' – clarify that 'did not do so' is the correct form.
6. Provide clear examples for each form, contextualized within digital work-life boundaries.
7. Lead students through a controlled practice task where they apply the structures.

Student handout

Mastering Concise Expression

To make our professional communication more efficient and sophisticated, we often use substitution and ellipsis to avoid repeating information. This helps streamline our messages and arguments.

Substitution with 'do so', 'not', 'too':
We use 'do so' (or 'did so', 'will do so', etc.) to replace an entire verb phrase that has just been used.
Original:* 'Many employees struggle with disconnecting, and management also struggles with disconnecting.'
Improved:* 'Many employees struggle with disconnecting, and management does so too.'

We can also use 'not' or 'too' with an auxiliary verb to avoid repeating a negative or affirmative statement.
Original:* 'I hope that the new policy won't be ignored. If the new policy isn't ignored, we will see positive changes.'
Improved:* 'I hope that the new policy won't be ignored. If not, we will see positive changes.'

T2

Test 2: Crafting a Policy Response

Teacher notes

1. Introduce the task: students will read an internal company memo and then draft a short, concise response.
2. Emphasize the importance of using the advanced structures (substitution and ellipsis) practiced in the TCH stage.
3. Instruct students to focus on professional register and to express their points clearly and concisely.
4. Encourage students to agree or disagree with specific points in the memo, using language that demonstrates advanced structural compaction.
5. Circulate and monitor, providing support and noting successful use of target structures.
6. For feedback, have students exchange their responses and identify examples of substitution/ellipsis.
7. Highlight strong examples on the board, demonstrating effective and concise professional communication.

Responding to Workplace Policies

Your company has just circulated a new internal memo regarding digital well-being and communication protocols. Read the excerpt below, then imagine you need to draft a short, professional response to a colleague or a superior, expressing agreement, disagreement, or a nuanced perspective.

*Internal Memo: Enhancing Digital Well-being and Communication*

*To ensure a healthier work-life balance and clearer communication channels, we are implementing new guidelines. Effective next month, employees are encouraged to avoid sending non-urgent emails outside of core business hours (9 AM - 5 PM). We believe this will foster a culture of respect for personal time. Furthermore, all departmental communication should prioritize official company platforms over personal messaging apps. If a team requires urgent out-of-hours contact, they must gain prior approval from their line manager to do so.*


Draft a short email (3-5 sentences) responding to this memo. In your response, ensure you:
* Express a clear opinion (agree, disagree, or partially agree).
* Use at least two examples of substitution or ellipsis (e.g., 'do so', 'if not', 'I hope not') to make your points concise and professional.
