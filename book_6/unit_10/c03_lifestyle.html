L-IN

Lead-In: Urban Planning and Your Daily Life

Teacher notes

Introduce the topic using the lead-in brief.
Divide students into pairs or small groups.
Monitor discussions, noting down interesting points and emergent language related to urban planning and work-life balance.

Student handout

City Life: How Design Affects You

Think about the design of your city or town, from its transport links to its green spaces. How do these elements of urban planning directly affect your daily life and your ability to balance work commitments with personal time?

Discuss in pairs or small groups:
1. What are the most important features of your local area for your daily life (e.g., transport, shops, parks)?
2. How easy is it to get to work and run errands? Does traffic or lack of public transport affect your work-life balance?
3. Are there enough green spaces or civic amenities nearby? How do these affect your stress levels or family time?
4. If you could change one thing about your city's planning to improve your daily life, what would it be and why?

MOD

Model Discussion: Evaluating City Amenities

Teacher notes

Students read the dialogue in pairs.
Ask students to identify key concerns raised by Maria and David.
Discuss briefly how the conversation relates to their own experiences.
Highlight any unknown vocabulary from the dialogue implicitly if needed, but do not pre-teach exhaustively.

Student handout

A Conversation About Our City

Read the conversation between Maria, a logistics worker, and David, who works in retail, as they discuss a new urban development:

Maria: Have you seen the plans for the new mixed-use development near the old factory site? They say it'll bring new shops and housing.
David: I did. On one hand, more local businesses could be good, especially if it means better civic amenities like a new health centre or community space. But on the other, will it truly improve our quality of life?
Maria: Exactly! My biggest concern is the public transport networks. My commute is already a nightmare. If they don't integrate the new area with efficient bus routes, it just means more cars on the road, more traffic. That directly impacts my work-life balance.
David: You're right. And what about green spaces? They're talking about high-density housing. We need more parks, not fewer, especially for families. Losing a relaxing spot makes a big difference after a long shift.
Maria: Absolutely. I hope they've thought about how people actually live and work here, not just building new things for the sake of it. Access to amenities that truly matter, like affordable shops and quick transport, is key for us.
David: Let's hope they listen to resident feedback. Good planning isn't just about buildings; it's about making daily life manageable and enjoyable.

FP

Fluency Practice: Urban Planning Proposal Discussion

Teacher notes

Divide students into groups of three.
Assign roles (A, B, C) within each group.
Explain the task clearly, emphasizing the need to express concerns, explain impacts, and propose solutions.
Remind students to use the phrases and vocabulary from the preparation stage.
Set a time limit (e.g., 10-12 minutes).
Monitor closely, noting down good language use and errors for delayed correction. Avoid interrupting fluency.

Student handout

Shaping Our City's Future

Imagine you are attending a public consultation meeting about a new urban development project in your city. The project aims to improve housing and commerce but has potential impacts on traffic, green spaces, and access to essential services.

Work in groups of three. Each person takes on one of these roles:

Role A: Local Resident / Employee: You live and work in the area. Your main priorities are good public transport networks, sufficient green spaces, and maintaining your work-life balance. You are concerned about increased traffic and potential loss of local character.

Role B: Small Business Owner: You run a local shop/service. You are interested in how mixed-use zoning could bring more customers but are also worried about competition from new businesses and whether civic amenities will keep up with population growth.

Role C: Community Organizer: You represent a local action group. Your focus is on the overall quality of life for residents, ensuring the development benefits everyone, especially the working class. You want to ensure affordable housing, good access to services, and community input.

Discuss the proposed project. Each person should clearly state their concerns, explain how the project might affect their daily life or business, and propose specific improvements or conditions for the project to go ahead.

Aim to reach a consensus or at least understand each other's perspectives on how to best develop the area.

FBDC

Feedback and Delayed Correction

Teacher notes

Start with open-class content feedback.
Ask each group to briefly report back on their main concerns, proposed solutions, or any consensus reached.
Address any general difficulties students encountered with the task.
Then, move to delayed error correction based on notes taken during the FP stage.
Write anonymized sentences on the board (good examples and errors).
Focus first on areas of clarity and effectiveness of communication.
Then, gently correct language errors, prompting students to self-correct where possible.
Reinforce the use of target vocabulary related to urban planning and work-life balance.
Conclude by asking students how they can apply this language in their own work or community discussions.

Student handout

Reflecting on Our City Discussion

Reflect on your discussion:
1. Did your group manage to understand each other's priorities?
2. What were the most challenging aspects of the discussion?
3. What solutions or compromises did your group come up with?
4. Did you feel confident expressing your opinions on urban planning issues?
