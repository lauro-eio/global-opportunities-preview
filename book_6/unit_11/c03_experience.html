L-IN

Lead-In: Understanding Team Dynamics

Teacher notes

1. Introduce the lesson theme: understanding communication styles for effective conflict resolution.
2. Present the hypothetical scenario from the lead-in brief.
3. Divide students into small groups or pairs.
4. Facilitate a group discussion based on the prompts provided, encouraging them to share initial thoughts and experiences.
5. Circulate and listen, noting any pre-existing knowledge or difficulties.

Student handout

Teamwork Challenges

Imagine your team is working on a crucial project. Two members, Anna and Ben, have very different working styles. Anna prefers detailed planning and strict deadlines, while Ben thrives on flexibility and spontaneous problem-solving. This is causing tension and delays.

Discuss the following with your group:

1. What are the potential conflicts that could arise from these different styles?
2. How might their individual strengths be used if they could communicate effectively?
3. What strategies could you use to help them understand each other's perspectives?

MOD

Model Dialogue: Resolving Workplace Conflict

Teacher notes

1. Tell students they will read a dialogue about resolving a similar conflict.
2. Instruct students to read the dialogue, paying attention to how the team leader and members communicate.
3. After reading, have students complete the comprehension tasks.
4. Review answers as a class, discussing the effective communication strategies demonstrated in the dialogue, such as active listening and empathy.

Student handout

A Productive Conversation

Read the following dialogue between a team leader, Chris, and two team members, Anna and Ben, who are trying to resolve a conflict over their differing work styles.

Chris: 'Anna, Ben, thanks for meeting. I understand there's been some friction regarding the project's workflow. I want to help us find a solution where everyone feels heard and productive.'

Anna: 'From my perspective, Ben's approach of waiting until the last minute makes me anxious and delays my parts of the project. I need to know the plan well in advance.'

Ben: 'I hear what you're saying, Anna. I understand that my spontaneous style can seem unorganized. However, I often find solutions quickly under pressure, and sometimes rigid plans limit innovation.'

Chris: 'Ben, you're saying that your methods, while appearing spontaneous, often lead to efficient problem-solving and can even foster creativity. Anna, you need structure and advance notice to perform your best and avoid stress. Active listening is key here. Can you both acknowledge each other's core needs?'

Anna: 'I can see how being too rigid might stifle new ideas, Ben. Perhaps I need to trust the process more.'

Ben: 'And I can definitely appreciate your need for a clear roadmap, Anna. It helps team cohesion if we're all on the same page. Maybe we can find a middle ground.'

Chris: 'Exactly. Finding that middle ground, through interpersonal skills and empathy at work, is our goal. How about we try a hybrid approach: detailed planning for the initial stages, but leave room for brainstorming and adapting during execution?'

PREP

Preparation: Phrases for Conflict Resolution

Teacher notes

1. Direct students to the column banks of useful phrases.
2. Explain that these phrases will help them express themselves and manage discussions in the next activity.
3. Encourage students to read through the phrases and choose a few they want to try to use.
4. Allow a few minutes for students to mentally prepare or make brief notes based on these phrases.

FP

Fluency Practice: Role-Playing Conflict Resolution

Teacher notes

1. Divide students into groups of three. If there's an uneven number, one group can be a pair with one student playing both conflicting roles, or the teacher can join a group as facilitator.
2. Assign roles (Role A, Role B, Facilitator) to each student within their groups.
3. Instruct them to read their roles carefully and then begin the discussion to find a resolution for an upcoming project.
4. Remind students to use the phrases from the PREP stage and to focus on active listening and empathy.
5. Circulate and monitor, taking notes on language use (effective phrases, general fluency, errors) for delayed correction in FBDC. Do not interrupt their speaking.

Student handout

Resolve the Conflict

Work in groups of three. One person will be a 'Facilitator' and two will be 'Team Members' with conflicting styles.

Role A (Team Member): You prioritize speed and getting tasks done quickly, even if it means cutting corners on detailed documentation. You believe results speak louder than detailed plans.

Role B (Team Member): You prioritize accuracy and detailed documentation. You believe thorough planning prevents mistakes and ensures quality, even if it takes more time.

Role C (Facilitator): Your job is to help Role A and Role B understand each other's needs and find a productive resolution that respects both approaches. Use the phrases from the previous activity.

Discuss the situation and try to agree on a new approach for an upcoming project. Remember to listen actively and show empathy.

FBDC

Feedback and Delayed Correction

Teacher notes

1. Content Feedback (5-7 minutes):
- Bring the class back together.
- Ask each group to share their proposed resolution and briefly explain how they reached it.
- Facilitate a whole-class discussion: What strategies did they find most effective? What were the challenges?
- Praise effective use of interpersonal skills and resolution strategies.
2. Language Feedback (8-10 minutes):
- Refer to notes taken during the FP stage.
- Write anonymized examples of good language use (e.g., specific phrases for showing understanding or proposing solutions) on the board.
- Then, address common errors observed in their speaking. Focus on areas that impacted clarity or fluency related to the lesson's target (e.g., incorrect prepositions in phrases, tense issues when discussing past conflict or future plans).
- Elicit corrections from students where possible.
- Reinforce the correct usage of target lexical items like active listening, empathy at work, and team cohesion if they were used incorrectly or could have been used more effectively.

Student handout

Reflect and Improve

Now that you've finished your role-plays, let's discuss what happened.

1. What was the solution your group agreed on?
2. What communication strategies did you find most useful in your role-play?
3. Was it challenging to understand the other person's perspective, and how did you overcome it?

We will also review some language points from your discussions to help improve your future communication.
