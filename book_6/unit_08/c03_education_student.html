Making Things Better at Work

We all encounter inefficiencies at work. Have you ever thought, 'There's a better way to do this'?

Discuss with a partner:
1. What is one specific task or process at your workplace (or a past one) that you believe could be more efficient or has recurring issues?
2. Briefly describe the problem and its impact.
3. Why do you think this inefficiency exists?
4. If you had the chance to propose a change, what might it be?

A Proposal for Change

Maria works in a logistics company. She has noticed a recurring problem with how packages are prepared for dispatch. Listen to her conversation with her manager, Mr. Davies, and answer the questions.

Maria: Good morning, Mr. Davies. Do you have a moment?
Mr. Davies: Good morning, Maria. Yes, come in.
Maria: Thank you. I wanted to discuss an observation regarding our package preparation process for the evening shift. I've noticed a significant amount of time is consistently lost due to the current manual labeling and sorting method.
Mr. Davies: Oh? Could you elaborate?
Maria: Certainly. Based on my observations over the past month, our team spends an average of 45 minutes each evening manually sorting packages that arrive unsorted from the morning delivery. This often leads to delays in loading, especially when there are unexpected high volumes.
Mr. Davies: I see. That's a considerable amount of time.
Maria: It is. I believe we could implement a small but effective change. My proposal is to introduce a pre-sorting station closer to the morning delivery intake point. This would involve the morning team doing a preliminary sort into designated bins as packages arrive, rather than them all being dumped in one area for the evening shift to handle.
Mr. Davies: That sounds like it could reduce the evening team's workload. What would the impact be on the morning team, though?
Maria: A valid point. I anticipate an additional 10-15 minutes of work for the morning shift. However, the overall efficiency gain for the entire day would be substantial. It would free up the evening team to focus on loading without the sorting backlog, potentially cutting evening prep time by 30-35 minutes. This would also improve our on-time dispatch rate, which has been a minor issue lately. I've put together some basic data from last month's logs to support this.
Mr. Davies: Interesting. So, a minor adjustment for morning staff to achieve a significant process optimization for the evening. And you have data to back this up?
Maria: Yes, I can show you the figures. I believe this change could lead to smoother operations and fewer overtime hours for the evening crew.
Mr. Davies: Thank you, Maria. This is a well-thought-out argument. Let's review your data. I appreciate you bringing this forward.

Building Your Argument

Now it's your turn to prepare a proposal. Think back to the inefficiency you discussed earlier. How would you present your idea to convince your manager or colleagues?

Use the phrases below to help you structure your proposal. Make notes on these points:

1. State the problem clearly: What is the issue? What impact does it have?
2. Propose a solution: What is your specific idea for change?
3. Support with evidence: Why is your solution good? What data, observations, or feedback can you use?
4. Address potential concerns: What questions or objections might your manager have? How will you respond?

Your Proposal, Your Presentation

Now it's time to put your planning into action! You will work in pairs.

Role A: The Presenter
You will present your proposal for a workplace improvement to your manager. Use your notes and the phrases from the previous activity to make a clear, persuasive argument. Remember to:
1. State the problem and its impact.
2. Propose your solution.
3. Support your solution with 'evidence' (observations, data, feedback).
4. Be ready to address any questions or concerns.

Role B: The Manager
You are the manager. Listen carefully to your colleague's proposal. Ask questions and raise concerns to understand the proposal fully and challenge it constructively. You might ask about:
* Cost or resources required.
* Impact on other teams or schedules.
* Potential risks or downsides.
* How the change would be implemented.

After Presenter A finishes, switch roles.

Reviewing Your Communication

Let's reflect on your proposals and presentations.

1. Thinking about the content:
* What were the strengths of the proposals you heard or presented?
* What might make a proposal even more convincing?

2. Thinking about the language:
* Did you find it easy to use phrases for stating problems, proposing solutions, and supporting your points?
* Were there any parts where you struggled with vocabulary or expressing your ideas clearly?

Your teacher will now give you some feedback on the language used during your presentations.
