L-IN

Lead-In: Cross-Border Commuting Challenges

Teacher notes

1. Introduce the scenario using the lead-in brief.
2. Divide students into pairs or small groups.
3. Ask them to discuss the questions provided.
4. Monitor discussions, noting any emerging language points or common challenges mentioned.
5. Facilitate a brief whole-class share-out of key points.

Student handout

Working Across the Line

Imagine an employee who lives in one country but works daily in an office just across the border in another country. Or consider someone working remotely from their home country for a company based abroad.

Discuss in pairs:

1. What specific logistical challenges might arise from this setup for the employee (e.g., travel, daily routine)?
2. What potential legal or policy challenges might the company face regarding this employee?
3. What about the employee themselves? What personal legal or administrative issues could they encounter (e.g., related to their income or where they live)?
4. Have you or anyone you know experienced something similar? What was the biggest challenge?

MOD

Model Dialogue: Navigating International Remote Work Policy

Teacher notes

1. Tell students they will read a conversation between two colleagues discussing a company's approach to cross-border work.
2. Have students read the dialogue individually or in pairs.
3. Ask them to answer the comprehension question that follows to check their understanding of the main topic.
4. Briefly discuss their answers as a class.

Student handout

Policy Talk

Read the conversation between Clara and David, two colleagues, about their company's new remote work guidelines.

Materials

The New International Remote Work Policy

Clara: Hey David, did you get a chance to look at the new international remote work policy?
David: Just skimmed it. It's quite detailed, especially the section on cross-border jurisdiction. I'm a bit worried about how it impacts Jane, who works for us from Portugal.
Clara: Exactly. It mentions specific visa requirements and work permit rules if an employee works from a country where we don't have a registered office. It seems more complex than just having a good internet connection.
David: Absolutely. And the tax implications for both the company and the employee are significant. Our HR team will have their hands full explaining how this affects everyone's residency status if they're not careful.
Clara: I think the biggest takeaway is that while remote work offers flexibility, doing it across borders requires a lot of clear policy and communication. It's not just a matter of personal choice; there are serious legal and financial considerations.
David: Agreed. We need to make sure everyone understands the boundaries and what support the company can offer. It's a tricky balance.

PREP

Preparation: Discussing Cross-Border Policy Issues

Teacher notes

1. Explain that students will prepare for a discussion or roleplay.
2. Direct students to the 'Key Policy Terms' and 'Useful Phrases' tables.
3. Advise them to think about how they might use these terms and phrases in a conversation about cross-border work.
4. Provide a minute or two for students to make brief notes based on the scenario, focusing on challenges and possible solutions.
5. Encourage them to prepare what they might say or ask.

FP

Fluency Practice: Policy Discussion Roleplay

Teacher notes

1. Set up the roleplay: Divide students into groups of three. Assign roles: Manager (or HR), Employee A (commutes daily), Employee B (works remotely from another country).
2. Explain the task clearly: The 'manager' must explain potential policy changes or gather feedback, while 'employees' raise concerns.
3. Emphasize that the goal is fluency and communicative success, not perfection.
4. Instruct students to use the key terms and phrases they prepared.
5. Monitor groups, listening for effective communication and the use of target language. Avoid interrupting unless communication breaks down.
6. Time the activity (e.g., 8-10 minutes per group).

Student handout

The Policy Meeting

You will now take part in a policy discussion. Work in groups of three. Assign roles:

Role A: Manager / HR Representative
You are leading a meeting to discuss a new or updated company policy on cross-border work. Your aim is to explain the company's position, clarify important details, and address employee concerns. You should be prepared to discuss work permits, visa requirements, tax implications, and residency status.

Role B: Employee (Cross-border commuter)
You live in one country but commute daily to work in a company office in another country. You have concerns about how the new policy might affect your daily routine, legal standing, and benefits.

Role C: Employee (International remote worker)
You work remotely from your home in a different country than the company's main office. You are worried about the cross-border jurisdiction aspects, your tax implications, and how the company will handle international remote work policy going forward.

Task: Have a meeting where the manager explains the policy considerations, and the employees raise their specific concerns and ask questions. Try to use the terms and phrases from the preparation stage.

FBDC

Feedback and Delayed Correction

Teacher notes

1. Content Feedback (5-7 minutes):
- Bring the class back together.
- Ask students to reflect on their discussions: What were the main challenges identified?
- How effectively did they manage to communicate their roles' perspectives?
- What were some interesting solutions or questions raised?
- Praise effective communication and successful negotiation of complex ideas.
2. Language Feedback (5-7 minutes):
- Refer to notes taken during the FP stage.
- Write common errors (not individual errors unless requested) on the board.
- Focus on errors related to clarity, fluency, and appropriate register, especially concerning the target vocabulary (e.g., incorrect usage of 'tax implications' or 'residency status').
- Elicit corrections from students or provide them as necessary. Do not spend too long on individual points; keep it focused on general improvement.
- Briefly highlight any examples of excellent language use observed during the task.
