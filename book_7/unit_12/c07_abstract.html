TCH

Clarification: Refining Rhetorical Logic

Teacher notes

1. Begin by reviewing the common issues observed in T1, without explicitly stating who made which errors.
2. Introduce the concept of 'Rhetorical Question Packaging for Advanced Strategic Logic' as a C2 structure.
3. Explain the meaning: Deploying high-register logical structures to highlight conceptual contradictions, formulate master paradigms, or emphasize systemic vulnerabilities in executive reports.
4. Present the form:
- Affirmative: Interrogative frame enclosing an axiomatic or declarative truth (e.g., 'How can the industry survive if its core ontology is invalid?')
- Negative: 'Is it not true that poor abstract reasoning compromises our epistemology safeguards?'
5. Use Concept Check Questions (CCQs):
- 'Are we genuinely seeking new data, or are we cementing an absolute strategic logic point?' (Expected answer: Cementing a strategic logic point)
- 'Does this pattern manipulate traditional grammar hierarchies for high-stakes professional engagement?' (Expected answer: Yes)
6. Provide clear examples contrasting incorrect and correct forms, drawing from the T1 errors.
7. Lead students through guided practice tasks, encouraging self-correction and peer feedback.

Student handout

Precision in Argumentation

In high-level communication, sometimes we don't ask questions to get new information, but to make an undeniable point. This is called Rhetorical Question Packaging for Advanced Strategic Logic.

Its purpose is to use a question format to highlight a fundamental truth, a serious contradiction, or a systemic vulnerability in formal reports and discussions.

Key Forms:
* Affirmative: An interrogative frame around an undeniable truth.
Example: _'How can a project succeed if its fundamental assumptions are flawed?'_
* Negative: Questioning a widely accepted negative truth to emphasize it.
Example: _'Is it not true that a lack of clear process undermines team efficiency?'_

Consider the examples below and refine them using these structures. Your goal is to make a definitive, strategic statement through the power of a well-formed question.

Teacher notes

1. Set up the scenario: Students are acting as consultants or senior analysts preparing a recommendation.
2. Instruct students to read the problem statement and, individually or in pairs, formulate a powerful rhetorical question using the target structure.
3. Emphasize that the question should crystallize a core insight or highlight a critical systemic issue, aligning with the meaning of the target language.
4. Encourage students to consider the impact and persuasive power of their chosen phrasing.
5. Review their formulated questions, focusing on the correct form and the effectiveness in conveying advanced strategic logic.
