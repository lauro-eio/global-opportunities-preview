L-IN

Lead-In: Commute Challenges

Teacher notes

1. Begin by asking learners about their commute to work on a typical day or the last time they faced a significant challenge getting to or from work.
2. Put learners in pairs or small groups.
3. Instruct them to discuss the questions, focusing on any 'last-mile' problems, missed connections, or difficulties accessing transport, especially for unusual shifts.
4. Encourage them to share specific examples of 'mobility friction' they've experienced.
5. Monitor for initial ideas and relevant vocabulary.

Student handout

Your Daily Journey

Think about your daily commute or any recent travel. Discuss with a partner or small group:

1. What are the biggest challenges you face getting to and from work?
2. Have you ever had a 'last-mile' problem – like missing a connection, not having a lift, or a blocked road near your destination?
3. How do your work shifts (early, late, night) affect your ability to access reliable transport?
4. What kind of impact do these transport issues have on your day or your work?

MOD

Model: Discussing Workplace Mobility

Teacher notes

1. Introduce the dialogue as a conversation between two colleagues, Maria and Ben, discussing their transport issues related to work.
2. Have learners read the dialogue silently first.
3. Ask learners to identify the specific problems Maria and Ben mention, and if they resonate with their own experiences.
4. Draw attention to phrases used to describe problems and their impact.
5. Clarify any new vocabulary if necessary through context.

Student handout

Workplace Transport Talk

Read the conversation between Maria and Ben, two colleagues discussing their travel to work:

Maria: Ben, rough morning! My bus was delayed by almost an hour, completely messing up my scheduling. It’s always an issue with the early shift; public transport access is so limited before 6 AM.

Ben: Tell me about it, Maria. I work late sometimes, and getting home is a nightmare. The company shuttle stops running at 10 PM, so I'm often left to figure out the last mile on my own. Taxis are expensive, and walking feels unsafe.

Maria: Exactly! It feels like the local infrastructure just isn't designed for people who don't work standard 9-to-5s. It impacts productivity when you're stressed before you even start.

Ben: Absolutely. I've even considered moving closer, but housing is so expensive. We really need a better solution for staff mobility outside peak hours.

Maria: I agree. It's a genuine problem for many of us.

PREP

Preparation: Planning Solutions

Teacher notes

1. Explain that learners will now prepare to discuss their own mobility challenges and potential solutions.
2. Direct learners to the 'Useful Phrases' and instruct them to use these to help articulate their ideas.
3. In pairs or individually, ask learners to brainstorm a specific 'last-mile' or commute problem relevant to their workplace or community.
4. Guide them to think about:
a. What is the problem?
b. What are its causes or contributing factors?
c. What are the consequences for individuals and the workplace?
d. What practical solutions could be proposed?
5. Encourage note-taking using the column bank structure as a guide, but without writing full sentences.

Student handout

Brainstorming Solutions

Now, prepare to discuss a 'last-mile' or commute problem you know well. Think about its causes, impacts, and possible solutions. Use the phrases below to help you structure your ideas. Make some notes, but don't write full sentences.

FP

Fluency Practice: Proposing Mobility Solutions

Teacher notes

1. Explain the scenario: Learners are now in a meeting to discuss and propose solutions for mobility issues faced by employees.
2. Put learners in small groups (3-4 participants) or new pairs.
3. Assign roles if appropriate (e.g., 'Team Leader', 'Problem Presenter', 'Solution Proposer') or allow for open discussion.
4. Remind learners to use the notes and useful phrases from the PREP stage.
5. Instruct them to clearly present the problem, discuss its impact, and collectively propose practical, workplace-dignified solutions.
6. Monitor for fluency, use of target vocabulary, and effective communication strategies. Do not interrupt for correction during this stage.

Student handout

The Mobility Meeting

Imagine you are in a team meeting. Your task is to discuss the common mobility access and last-mile problems that employees in your company face, especially those on varied shifts or living in areas with poor transport links.

Working in small groups:

1. Each person should briefly present a specific problem they or colleagues experience.
2. As a group, discuss the root causes and consequences of these problems.
3. Brainstorm and propose practical, realistic solutions that your company could implement to improve employee mobility and access to the workplace.
4. Aim to reach a consensus on the best one or two solutions to present to senior management. Focus on clear communication and professional language.

FBDC

Feedback and Delayed Correction

Teacher notes

1. Content Feedback: Start by asking groups to share the problems they discussed and the solutions they proposed. Focus on the practicality and relevance of their ideas.
2. Language Feedback: After content feedback, provide delayed language correction. Refer to notes taken during the FP stage.
3. Focus on:
a. Any pronunciation issues that hindered communication.
b. Effective use of vocabulary related to 'mobility access' and 'last-mile problems' ('scheduling', 'infrastructure', 'access', 'mobility').
c. Fluency and coherence in expressing ideas.
d. Appropriate professional register.
4. Write common errors on the board without attributing them to specific individuals and elicit corrections from the class.
5. Praise good examples of communicative language and successful problem-solving discussions.

Student handout

Reflecting on Solutions

Think about your discussion in the 'Mobility Meeting':

1. Did your group effectively identify and describe the key mobility problems?
2. Were your proposed solutions practical and realistic for a workplace?
3. How well did you communicate your ideas and listen to your colleagues?

Listen to your teacher's feedback on the content of your discussion and the language you used.
