L-IN

Lead-In

Teacher notes

1. Divide students into small groups (3-4).
2. Introduce the topic by reading the lead-in brief aloud.
3. Ask groups to discuss the questions, encouraging them to share personal experiences or observations related to purchasing goods and worker conditions.
4. Facilitate a brief whole-class discussion, drawing out key points and initial opinions.

Student handout

The Cost of Convenience

When we buy products, it's easy to focus on the price or convenience. But what about the people who make them? Their working conditions, their health, their safety – how much do you think about these? Let's discuss:

1. When you're shopping for clothes, electronics, or everyday items, how often do you consider where and how they were made?
2. Do you believe companies have a moral or ethical obligation to ensure safe and healthy working conditions for all workers in their entire supply chain, not just those in their direct employment?
3. What are some practical challenges companies might face in doing this?
4. As consumers, what power do we have to influence these practices?

MOD

Model Dialogue: Supply Chain Ethics

Teacher notes

1. Tell students they will read a dialogue between two managers discussing a new ethical sourcing policy.
2. Ask them to read the dialogue individually.
3. Instruct them to pay attention to how the speakers express their views and the vocabulary used to discuss ethical responsibility.
4. After reading, check for general understanding of the managers' positions.

Student handout

An Ethical Dilemma in Practice

Read the following conversation between Maria, a Sustainability Officer, and David, a Procurement Manager, discussing their company's updated ethical sourcing policy.

Maria: David, I wanted to discuss the new ethical sourcing policy. Specifically, how we're going to implement supply chain transparency across all our tiers.

David: Maria, it's certainly ambitious. Our legal team is worried about the feasibility, especially concerning smaller, indirect suppliers. How can we truly ensure labor standards and worker health and safety in places we don't directly control?

Maria: That's the core challenge of corporate social responsibility. It's not just about direct control; it's about our ethical footprint. We need to audit beyond our tier-one suppliers. If we're profiting from goods, we have a responsibility to ensure they're not produced through exploitation or unsafe practices.

David: I agree in principle, but the cost implications are significant. We're talking about extensive third-party audits, potential training for partners, and possibly even severing ties with long-standing suppliers who can't meet the new benchmarks. Our shareholders expect returns, not just moral victories.

Maria: But isn't long-term reputation and brand integrity also a return? Ignoring these issues risks boycotts, legal challenges, and a loss of consumer trust. Plus, many of these issues, like basic health provisions or proper safety gear, are non-negotiable human rights. It's about protecting lives, not just profits.

David: So, how do we balance the imperative for ethical sourcing with maintaining competitive pricing and operational efficiency?

Maria: It requires a multi-pronged approach: clearer contracts, incentivizing compliance, and building stronger relationships with suppliers to support their transition. It's a journey, not a switch. Our reputation, and our workers' well-being, depend on it.

PREP

Preparation

Teacher notes

1. Explain that students will prepare for a discussion on corporate ethical responsibility.
2. Divide students into two teams (or assign roles): one advocating for stronger corporate ethical responsibility in supply chains, and the other highlighting the challenges and practical limitations.
3. Instruct students to use the column banks to gather arguments and phrases.
4. Encourage them to make notes in preparation for the debate, considering examples or real-world scenarios.

Student handout

Building Your Argument

You are going to take part in a discussion about the ethical responsibility of companies. Work with a partner or in a small group to prepare your arguments. You will either argue for stronger corporate ethical responsibility or discuss the practical challenges and limitations.

Use the phrases below to help you structure your points and express your opinions. Make notes on the key points you want to make.

What are your strongest arguments? What counter-arguments might you face?

FP

Free Practice: Ethical Supply Chain Debate

Teacher notes

1. Divide the class into two opposing groups (or pairs if the class is small) based on their preparation in the previous stage.
2. Clearly state the objective: to discuss the ethical responsibility of companies regarding worker health and safety in their supply chains, aiming to present convincing arguments.
3. Give a time limit for the discussion (e.g., 10-15 minutes).
4. Circulate, monitoring for fluency, communicative effectiveness, and appropriate use of vocabulary related to the theme. Avoid interrupting to correct.

Student handout

The Boardroom Discussion

Imagine you are managers at a large multinational company. Your board is discussing a new proposal to significantly increase investment in monitoring and improving worker health and safety standards across your entire global supply chain.

Your Task: Engage in a discussion where one side advocates strongly for this increased ethical responsibility, emphasizing its necessity and benefits, while the other side highlights the practical challenges, financial pressures, and potential competitive disadvantages.

Try to:
* Use the vocabulary and phrases from the previous preparation stage.
* Listen to and respond to each other's points.
* Aim for a balanced discussion, even if you represent one side.

FBDC

Feedback and Delayed Correction

Teacher notes

1. Content Feedback (5-7 minutes): Begin by asking groups to share the main points of their discussions. What were the strongest arguments? Did anyone change their mind? What solutions or compromises were suggested? Focus on the ideas and opinions first.
2. Language Feedback (5-7 minutes): Transition to language feedback. Write up a selection of common errors (pronunciation, lexical choice, fluency issues) observed during the FP stage on the board. Work through these as a class, eliciting corrections from students where possible. Do not single out individuals for errors.
3. Positive Reinforcement: Highlight examples of good language use, especially successful communication of complex ideas related to ethical sourcing, supply chain transparency, or corporate social responsibility.
4. Briefly review the key vocabulary from the lesson.

Student handout

Reflecting on Our Stance

Let's reflect on our discussion. What were the most challenging aspects of arguing your side? Did you hear any particularly strong arguments from the other team?

Consider:
* Which arguments were most convincing?
* Were there any points of agreement or potential compromise?
* What new insights did you gain about ethical supply chains or corporate responsibility?
