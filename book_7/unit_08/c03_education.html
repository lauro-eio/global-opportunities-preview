L-IN

Lead-In: Strategic Knowledge Transfer

Teacher notes

1. Set the context for the lesson, referring to the lead-in brief.
2. Divide students into pairs or small groups.
3. Ask students to discuss the questions, encouraging them to share personal experiences from their workplaces.
4. Monitor the discussions, listening for existing strategies and potential challenges in peer instruction.
5. Bring the class back together for a brief whole-group share-out of ideas.

Learning from Experience

Read the conversation between Elena and Mark. They are discussing how to train a new team member.

Materials

Mark and Elena's Training Discussion

Elena: Mark, I’m a bit worried about training Liam on the new inventory system next week. It’s pretty complex.
Mark: I hear you. It can be tough. What’s your initial plan?
Elena: Well, I thought I’d just walk him through it step-by-step, then let him try.
Mark: That’s a start, but for onboarding efficiency, I’ve found a few instructional techniques really help. First, break it down. Don’t overwhelm him with everything at once. Show him the most critical part, then let him do it immediately with you watching.
Elena: So, practical application right away?
Mark: Exactly. And for effective knowledge sharing, instead of just telling him what to do, explain why it’s done that way. Understanding the purpose helps retention.
Elena: That makes sense. I often forget the 'why'.
Mark: Also, after he tries, don't just say 'good job'. Ask him to explain the steps back to you. It's a great way to check his understanding and identify any gaps. This kind of peer coaching is invaluable.
Elena: Right, so 'teach it back' instead of just 'show and tell'.
Mark: And share best practices dissemination as you go. Point out common mistakes and how to avoid them. By the end, the goal is for him to feel independent, not just follow instructions.
Elena: That’s a lot more strategic than my original plan. Thanks, Mark. I'll definitely use these tips.

PREP

Preparation: Structuring Your Training Plan

Teacher notes

1. Explain that students will now prepare for a role-play where they train a new colleague.
2. Direct students to the column banks, explaining that these phrases can help them structure their training approach.
3. Give students time (5-7 minutes) to review the phrases and make brief notes for a hypothetical complex procedure they might teach. Emphasize that they don't need a detailed script, just a plan for how they would teach it, using the provided strategies and language.
4. Circulate, offering support and clarifying any phrases.

FP

Free Practice: Designing a Training Session

Teacher notes

1. Explain the role-play scenario clearly, assigning Role A (Experienced Colleague) and Role B (New Colleague, Pat).
2. Emphasize that students should focus on how they would conduct the training, applying the strategies discussed and using phrases from the preparation stage.
3. Give students 10-12 minutes for the role-play. Circulate and monitor, taking notes on language (e.g., strong usage of target lexis, errors for delayed correction) and content (e.g., effective use of strategies).
4. Do not interrupt during the activity; simply note down observations.

Student handout

Role Play: Guiding Your New Colleague

Work in pairs. You will role-play a training session.

Role A: The Experienced Colleague
You are tasked with training a new team member, Pat, on a complex, multi-step procedure vital to your team's output (e.g., operating a specific piece of machinery, a complex software process, or a critical safety protocol). Your goal is to ensure Pat truly grasps it and can perform independently. Focus on your instructional techniques.

Role B: Pat, the New Colleague
You are new to the team and need to learn a complex procedure. Ask questions, try to understand the 'why' behind the steps, and show your willingness to learn and apply the information. Encourage knowledge sharing.

Task:
Have a conversation where Role A begins to train Role B on a procedure. Focus on implementing effective instructional techniques, knowledge sharing, and demonstrating peer coaching. Aim to make Pat feel confident and capable. You do not need to invent a full procedure, just discuss how you would teach and learn a key part of it.

FBDC

Feedback and Delayed Correction

Teacher notes

1. Content Feedback (5-7 mins):
* Open with general positive feedback on students' engagement and use of strategies.
* Ask pairs to share their answers to the reflection questions, focusing on the effectiveness of their training strategies and communicative approach.
* Encourage students to provide specific examples from their role-play.
2. Language Feedback (3-5 mins):
* Write key errors (lexis, pronunciation, fluency blockers) noted during the FP stage on the board.
* Elicit corrections from students for each error.
* Provide correct models where necessary and drill if useful.
* Highlight examples of successful use of target lexis and phrases (e.g., 'onboarding efficiency', 'best practices dissemination', 'peer coaching').
* Summarize the key takeaways regarding effective communication in training.
